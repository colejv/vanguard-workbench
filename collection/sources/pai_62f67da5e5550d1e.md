Last year, MIU’s Marine Coders team delivered two plugins for the Android Tactical Assault Kit (ATAK): HLZ Predictor, which provides potential helicopter landing zones based on terrain coverage and elevation data, and Flight Mode, which optimizes the TAK user interface for in-flight use and automizes the receipt of 9-Line communications.
Both applications were developed through both independent effort and team-based two-week coding “sprints” in partnership with Marine Corps Software Factory and Marine Corps Tactical Systems Support Activity (MCTSSA). Together with operational testing and user feedback from Marine Aviation Weapons and Tactics Squadron One (MAWTS-1), MIU Marines rapidly and continuously iterated on and improved the software they were developing.
As we look forward to 2026, the Marine Coders plan to release updated versions of both plugins with additional features and refinements, including leveraging different topographical datasets and adding functionality to support casualty evacuation operations.
HLZ Predictor and Flight Mode are available for download and immediate use from the Tactical Assault Kit Plug-Ins catalog at https://tak.gov/plugins

Zac’s point is well taken.
For more than a century, the training and qualification of junior officers standing watch as Officer of the Deck (OOD) followed a clear chain of custody: the Commanding Officer’s personal trust in a specific human being. That trust was not abstract. It was earned through structured education, supervised watch standing, qualification boards, written and practical examinations, and most importantly - direct accountability. When a CO signed an OOD letter, they were staking their command, their career, and the safety of their crew on that officer’s demonstrated competence.
The cartoon makes the joke uncomfortably clear. A cheerful “yes” from an unverified system, followed by regret after the fact, is exactly what happens when trust is asserted rather than engineered. With human watchstanders, we never relied on optimism alone. We built certification pipelines, standards, audits, and consequences. We did this precisely because navigation or command errors cost lives, damages or sinks ships, and deminishes national credibility. Human error is expected and managed and is certainly not unique.
AI-enabled autonomy raises the stakes further, not lowers them. Unlike a junior officer, an AI system operates at machine speed, at global scale, and across a contested cyber domain. Its failure modes are not just fatigue or inexperience, but data poisoning, model drift, spoofing, latent software defects, supply-chain compromise, and adversarial manipulation. In that environment, “trusting the AI” without an explicit assessment, certification, and continuous assurance framework is not innovation it is negligence by another name.
Cybersecurity and autonomy are inseparable. An autonomous navigation system that cannot prove data integrity, decision provenance, resilience to deception, and bounded behavior under uncertainty is no more qualified than an Ensign who has never stood a watch. We would never allow the latter to take the deck; we should not allow the former to take control of a vessel flying the U.S. flag.
The uncomfortable truth is that autonomy needs its own equivalent of OOD qualification: rigorous testing, red-teaming, certification authorities, lifecycle recertification, and clear command accountability for when and why trust is granted. Until that infrastructure exists, every “yes” from an AI system should be treated as a hypothesis to be verified, not an answer to be accepted.
Zac and Dr. Cara LaPointe are right to raise this now. The future they’re warning about is avoidable but only if we apply the same seriousness to AI trust and autonomy infrastructure staring with the operating system that generations of naval leaders applied to watch standing, navigation, command responsibility and as Officers of the Deck.
